Cyber insurance used to be a straightforward checkbox exercise: answer a short questionnaire, pay a premium, get a policy. That changed significantly by 2026. Applications now function closer to an audit than a form, insurers increasingly verify what applicants claim, and a documented gap between what you said on the application and what you actually enforce can mean a denied claim exactly when you need coverage most.

## Why this shifted

The driver is straightforward: ransomware claims have been large and frequent enough that insurers tightened underwriting to stay profitable, rather than simply raising prices uniformly across all applicants. The practical result is that two businesses with identical risk profiles on paper can get very different outcomes depending on how well they can actually demonstrate — not just claim — the controls insurers now expect.

Market-wide, this is also showing up in pricing: one 2026 forecast puts premium increases across the cyber insurance market at roughly 15–20% for the year, reflecting the broader claims environment rather than any single applicant's controls.

## What insurers expect to see in 2026

Across current guidance from brokers, MSPs, and insurance-focused advisories, a consistent baseline set of controls shows up repeatedly as what underwriters now expect, not just prefer:

- **Multi-factor authentication, comprehensively** — not just on some systems, but specifically on remote access, VPN connections, privileged and admin accounts, and email. Partial MFA coverage is a common gap that surfaces during claims review.
- **Endpoint detection and response (EDR) on every device** — a step up from traditional antivirus, reflecting how much of the broader threat landscape (including AI-accelerated attacks) has shifted toward behaviors signature-based tools miss.
- **Immutable, tested offsite backups** — not just backups that exist, but backups an organization has actually verified it can restore from, stored in a way ransomware operating inside the network can't also encrypt or delete.
- **A written incident response plan** — documented, not informal institutional knowledge that leaves with whichever employee happens to know the process.
- **Security awareness training**, on an ongoing basis rather than a one-time onboarding checkbox.

## Why "we have MFA" isn't the same as "MFA is enforced everywhere"

The sharpest and most consequential shift in 2026 underwriting is the gap between what an application claims and what gets verified — sometimes only after a claim is filed. A documented pattern: an organization answers "yes" to MFA on its application, honestly believing it to be accurate, only for a post-incident review to find a single privileged account that was exempted for convenience, or a legacy system where MFA was never actually configured despite policy saying it should be.

One advisory specifically frames the risk this way: an insurer can reject a claim in whole or in part if MFA was represented as enforced on the application but was found to be absent on the specific account or system where the loss occurred — even if the overall organization-wide MFA coverage was mostly accurate.

The practical implication is that "we have a policy requiring MFA" and "MFA is actually enforced on every account, verifiably, including the ones nobody thinks about" are different claims — and only the second one holds up under the scrutiny a real claim now receives.

## Evidence matters as much as the controls themselves

Multiple 2026 sources converge on the same practical advice: **keep evidence for every control you claim**, not just the control itself. Configuration exports showing MFA enforcement scope, EDR deployment records, backup test logs with restoration timestamps, and a dated, version-controlled incident response plan all matter — not because insurers demand paperwork for its own sake, but because a claim dispute turns into exactly this kind of documentation review, and the business without it is at a structural disadvantage regardless of how good its actual security posture was.

## A practical pre-application checklist

Before applying for or renewing cyber insurance in 2026, it's worth treating the process as a mini internal audit rather than a form to fill out quickly:

- **Verify MFA coverage directly**, account by account, for remote access, VPN, admin/privileged accounts, and email — don't rely on "we have a policy for this."
- **Confirm EDR is actually deployed and reporting** on every endpoint, not just licensed for a subset.
- **Test your backup restoration process**, not just confirm backups are running — an untested backup is a hypothesis, not a control.
- **Write or update your incident response plan**, with named roles and a realistic communication plan, not a generic template nobody's reviewed since it was created.
- **Document everything** as you go — screenshots, configuration exports, test logs — so you have evidence ready if a claim or renewal review asks for it.
- **Answer the application honestly, even where the honest answer is "partially"** — a gap disclosed upfront is a known underwriting factor; a gap discovered during a claim is a potential denial.

## Cyber insurance readiness checklist

- [ ] Verify MFA enforcement account-by-account on remote access, VPN, admin accounts, and email
- [ ] Confirm EDR is deployed and actively reporting on every endpoint, not just licensed
- [ ] Test backup restoration, not just confirm backups run — document the test
- [ ] Write or update a specific, named-roles incident response plan
- [ ] Deliver ongoing (not one-time) security awareness training
- [ ] Keep documented evidence for every control claimed on the application
- [ ] Answer applications honestly, including partial or in-progress controls
- [ ] Re-verify all of the above before each renewal, not just at initial application

## Key takeaways

Cyber insurance underwriting in 2026 has shifted from a form to something closer to an audit, and the gap between what an application claims and what's actually enforced is now a real source of denied or reduced claims — not a theoretical risk. The baseline expectation (comprehensive MFA, EDR everywhere, tested backups, a written IR plan, ongoing training) is achievable for most SMEs, but only if it's genuinely enforced and documented, not just policy on paper.
