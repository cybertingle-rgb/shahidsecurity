Google announced on **November 16, 2026** that it is splitting Gemini's single privacy toggle into two separate controls — one governing **how long chat history is kept**, and a second, independent one governing **whether chat content can be used to train Google's AI models**. Buried in the same change: conversations with history turned off, previously retained for just 72 hours, now persist for roughly **two months**. If anyone at your business uses Gemini — officially sanctioned or not — this is a genuine policy change to your data's actual lifecycle, not a cosmetic settings update.

## Why "we turned history off" doesn't mean what it used to

The old assumption many businesses operated on — "if an employee doesn't want a chat retained, they turn history off, and it's handled" — was always a simplification, and this change makes that gap more consequential:

- **Retention for history-off chats jumped from 72 hours to roughly two months** — a nearly 20x increase in how long that content actually exists on Google's servers, even when a user believed they'd opted for minimal retention.
- **Training consent and retention are now genuinely separate decisions** — a business can no longer assume that disabling one setting handled both concerns at once, which means an employee (or an IT policy) set up under the old single-toggle model may now be in a different privacy posture than intended, without anyone having changed anything.
- **Workspace (business/school) accounts may see different defaults depending on admin configuration** — meaning the outcome for your organization specifically depends on settings your IT administrator controls, not just what an individual employee sees in their own account.

## The real risk isn't Google's policy — it's not knowing your own

This is the same structural problem our [Shadow AI risks guide](/blog/shadow-ai-security-risks/) and [AI acceptable-use policy guide](/blog/ai-acceptable-use-policy-business-guide/) both cover from different angles: most businesses don't have a clear, current answer to "what AI tools does our team actually use, under what settings, with what data." A platform-level privacy change like this one doesn't create risk on its own — it exposes whichever businesses never had a real answer to that question in the first place.

## What to do now

- **If your business uses Google Workspace, have your administrator confirm the Gemini defaults that rolled out for your organization** specifically — don't assume individual employee settings or old policy documentation still reflect reality.
- **Update your AI acceptable-use policy to reflect the new two-month history-off retention figure explicitly**, if your policy previously cited the old 72-hour window as a reason Gemini was considered low-risk for sensitive discussions.
- **Treat any client-confidential, legally privileged, or regulated data discussed through Gemini as retained for up to two months even with history off**, and set internal guidance accordingly — don't rely on the old assumption.
- **Confirm whether your training-data opt-out setting is actually configured the way your policy assumes**, now that it's a genuinely separate control from retention — the two no longer move together automatically.

## Gemini privacy policy update checklist

- [ ] Confirm your organization's actual Gemini retention and training-data settings post-November 16, 2026, rather than assuming prior configuration carried over as expected
- [ ] Update internal AI acceptable-use policy documentation to reflect the new two-month history-off retention window
- [ ] Brief staff who use Gemini that "history off" no longer means near-immediate deletion
- [ ] Verify Workspace admin-level defaults for Gemini match your organization's actual data-handling requirements
- [ ] Review whether any regulated or client-confidential data has been discussed through Gemini under the old retention assumption, and whether that needs addressing

## Key takeaways

Google's Gemini privacy split looks like a routine settings update, but it quietly rewrote a number — 72 hours to roughly two months — that a lot of businesses' informal AI policies were built on without anyone writing it down. If your organization's AI governance exists mostly as an assumption rather than a reviewed policy, this is a concrete, dated reason to make it a real document instead.
