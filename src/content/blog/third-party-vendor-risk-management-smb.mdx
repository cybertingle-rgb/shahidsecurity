Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report found that breaches involving a third party had jumped to roughly 30% of all breaches analyzed, roughly double the prior year's figure. Separately, Black Kite's 2026 Third-Party Breach Report found that a single vendor breach now affects an average of more than five downstream companies, with an average disclosure delay of well over three months between the vendor's breach and public notification. Put together: your vendors are a real, growing part of your own attack surface, and you often won't hear about a problem quickly when one happens.

Most small and mid-size businesses don't have a dedicated third-party risk management (TPRM) function — and they don't need one to meaningfully reduce this risk. What they need is a lightweight, consistently-applied process.

## Why vendor risk is really your risk

When a vendor has access to your customer data, your network, or your financial systems, their security posture becomes a direct extension of yours — regardless of whose logo is on the breach notification. A payroll provider with weak access controls, a marketing agency with standing admin access to your website, or a contractor using a shared, unmanaged password all create exposure that a firewall around your own office never touches.

This isn't hypothetical risk-management language. It's the specific, documented pattern behind a large share of recent breaches: the attacker doesn't go through the front door of the company they're actually after — they go through a vendor's weaker, less-monitored front door instead.

## A practical vendor-risk tiering approach

Not every vendor deserves the same scrutiny. A cleaning service that never touches your systems is a different risk category from a SaaS platform holding your customer database. A simple three-tier model works for most SMBs:

- **Tier 1 — Critical access.** Vendors with access to customer data, financial systems, source code, or your network/infrastructure (your CRM provider, your accounting software, your IT/MSP provider, your developers). These get full due diligence before onboarding and an annual review.
- **Tier 2 — Limited access.** Vendors with access to a narrower slice of data or systems (a marketing tool with contact lists, a scheduling tool with calendar access). These get a lighter review — a security questionnaire and a check on whether they publish any security certifications — and a review every one to two years.
- **Tier 3 — No meaningful access.** Vendors with no access to your systems or sensitive data (office supplies, physical services). Standard commercial due diligence only; no security-specific review needed.

Tiering isn't bureaucracy for its own sake — it's what makes the process sustainable. A small business that tries to apply enterprise-grade due diligence to every vendor, regardless of access level, abandons the process within a quarter. A tiered approach keeps the real scrutiny where it actually matters.

## What to actually ask a Tier 1 vendor

A security questionnaire doesn't need to be fifty pages to be useful. For a critical vendor, focus on questions with concrete, checkable answers:

- **Do you have a current SOC 2 Type II or ISO/IEC 27001 certification?** If yes, request the actual report (or a summary letter) rather than taking the claim at face value, and check its scope — some certifications cover only a subset of a vendor's product or infrastructure.
- **Where is our data stored, and who can access it?** You want a specific answer (cloud provider, region, named internal roles with access) — not "it's secure."
- **How do you handle a breach that affects our data, and how fast will you tell us?** This is where contract terms matter as much as the verbal answer — see below.
- **Do you encrypt data at rest and in transit?** A basic, binary-answerable question that still surprises how often the answer is unclear or evasive.
- **What happens to our data if we terminate the relationship?** Deletion timelines and data-return processes are frequently undocumented until you ask.
- **Do you use subprocessors or subcontractors who would also touch our data?** A vendor's own security posture doesn't cover risk introduced by who *they* outsource to.

## Contract and data-handling terms that actually matter

Due diligence before signing is only half the picture — the contract itself is where protection becomes enforceable rather than a verbal assurance.

For any vendor handling customer data (especially personal data covered by GDPR, UK data protection law, or similar regimes in your target markets), look for:

- **A data processing agreement (DPA)** defining what the vendor can do with your data, how long they retain it, and their obligations if they experience a breach.
- **A specific breach-notification timeframe** — "promptly" is not a timeframe; look for a defined number of hours or days.
- **The right to audit or request evidence of security controls**, not just a one-time questionnaire at signing.
- **Clear data deletion/return terms** on contract termination.
- **Limitation-of-liability language that doesn't entirely shield the vendor** from the consequences of their own security failures.

None of this requires a specialist lawyer for every vendor relationship — but it's worth one genuine legal review of your standard vendor contract template, since the same clauses will then apply across most future vendor relationships without repeated negotiation.

## Ongoing monitoring, not just onboarding

The highest-risk assumption in vendor risk management is treating it as a one-time gate at signing. A vendor's security posture isn't static — a company that passed your review two years ago may have since changed ownership, cut security staff, or suffered an undisclosed incident.

For Tier 1 vendors specifically:

- Re-review annually, not just at initial onboarding.
- Ask whether their certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001) are still current — these expire and require renewal audits.
- Watch public breach-notification and security-news sources for mentions of your critical vendors.
- Reassess the relationship whenever a vendor changes ownership, has a public security incident, or materially changes what they do with your data.

## Vendor risk checklist

- [ ] List every vendor with access to your data, systems, or network
- [ ] Tier each vendor by what they can actually access (critical / limited / none)
- [ ] For Tier 1 vendors: request current SOC 2/ISO 27001 evidence, not just a claim
- [ ] Confirm a DPA and a specific breach-notification timeframe exist in the contract
- [ ] Confirm data deletion/return terms for contract termination
- [ ] Set a recurring annual review date for every Tier 1 vendor
- [ ] Note which vendors use subprocessors that would also touch your data

## Key takeaways

Third-party involvement in breaches has grown sharply — Verizon's 2025 DBIR put it at roughly double the prior year's rate — and a single vendor breach now tends to ripple out to multiple downstream companies with a real lag before anyone finds out. A small business doesn't need an enterprise TPRM platform to manage this risk meaningfully; it needs a tiered vendor list, a short set of concrete due-diligence questions for its highest-access vendors, enforceable contract terms, and a recurring annual review — not a one-time check at signing.
