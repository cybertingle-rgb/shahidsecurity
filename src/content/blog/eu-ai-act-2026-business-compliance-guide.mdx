The EU AI Act stopped being a future consideration on **August 2, 2026**, when its core obligations — including rules for general-purpose AI models and governance requirements — became enforceable, with national authorities now empowered to act and maximum fines reaching **€35 million or 7% of global annual turnover**, whichever is higher. If your business has spent the past year treating this as a "we'll deal with it later" compliance item, later has already arrived.

## Why this matters even if you are not based in the EU

The EU AI Act follows the same extraterritorial pattern GDPR set years ago: it is not primarily about where your company is incorporated, it is about whose outcomes your AI system affects. A business anywhere in the world can be caught as a **"provider"** (if it builds or substantially modifies an AI system placed on the EU market) or a **"deployer"** (if it uses an AI system whose output affects people in the EU), regardless of headquarters location.

This lands squarely on the kind of business reading this article: software exporters and SaaS companies with EU customers, AI-feature vendors selling into European markets, and any company using a third-party AI tool whose output touches an EU employee or customer — a recruitment screening tool used to evaluate an EU-based applicant, for instance, or a credit-scoring feature used by a European customer of your platform.

## The risk tiers, in practical terms

The Act does not regulate "AI" as one category — it tiers obligations by risk:

- **Unacceptable risk** — a short list of practices banned outright (certain biometric categorization, manipulative AI techniques, social scoring). Most legitimate businesses are nowhere near this tier.
- **High-risk** — systems used in hiring and employee evaluation, credit and insurance scoring, education access, law enforcement, and safety-critical infrastructure carry the heaviest obligations: risk management systems, data governance documentation, human oversight, and conformity assessment before deployment.
- **Limited risk** — systems like chatbots and emotion-recognition tools carry mainly transparency obligations (disclosing that a user is interacting with AI).
- **Minimal risk** — the large majority of everyday business AI tools (spam filters, internal drafting assistants, recommendation features) carry essentially no specific obligation under the Act beyond general good practice.

The practical first step for almost every business is simply classifying its actual AI use honestly against these tiers — a step a surprising number of companies skip, either assuming the Act does not apply to them at all, or assuming every AI tool they touch must be high-risk by default. Both assumptions tend to be wrong, and both lead to the wrong next action.

## What has — and has not — changed since August

Two things are worth being precise about, because reporting on this has been inconsistent:

- **The headline date held.** Despite ongoing discussion in Brussels about a "Digital Omnibus" proposal that would push back some high-risk-system deadlines (to as late as 2027–2028 for certain categories), that proposal had not been formally adopted as of this writing. The general obligations and governance rules that took effect August 2, 2026 remain the operative deadline unless and until a delay is formally signed into law — a business should not treat a proposed delay as a granted one.
- **Enforcement is now a national-authority function, not a future promise.** Market surveillance authorities in EU member states can now investigate and act on high-risk systems — recruitment software and credit scoring being the most commonly cited examples — which changes this from a documentation exercise into one with a live regulator on the other end.

This connects directly to the internal governance work covered in our guide on [writing an AI acceptable use policy](/blog/ai-acceptable-use-policy-business-guide/) — an AUP governs how your own staff use AI tools; EU AI Act compliance governs what obligations attach to the AI systems your business builds, sells, or deploys that touch EU data subjects. Most businesses need both, and they are not the same document.

## A realistic compliance starting point for an SME

- **Build an AI system inventory** — every AI tool or feature in active use, who built it (you, or a vendor), and what decision or output it influences.
- **Classify each one against the risk tiers** honestly, rather than defensively over- or under-classifying.
- **Identify your role** for each system — provider, deployer, or neither — since the obligations differ meaningfully between them.
- **Check vendor documentation** for any third-party AI tool you deploy; a high-risk-tier vendor tool should be providing you the documentation needed to meet your deployer obligations, and a vendor who cannot produce it is a real due-diligence red flag, not a minor gap.
- **Document your governance process**, even a lightweight one — a written record that you assessed risk tier and obligations is itself part of what a regulator or an EU enterprise customer's procurement team will ask to see.

## EU AI Act readiness checklist

- [ ] Inventory every AI system or AI-powered feature the business builds, sells, or uses
- [ ] Classify each system's risk tier (unacceptable / high / limited / minimal) honestly
- [ ] Determine your role (provider, deployer, both, or neither) for each system
- [ ] For any high-risk system, confirm documentation, human oversight, and risk-management processes exist
- [ ] Request compliance documentation from any third-party AI vendor feeding a high-risk use case
- [ ] Track the Digital Omnibus proposal's actual legal status rather than assuming a delay has already been granted
- [ ] Review this alongside your internal AI acceptable use policy — the two cover different obligations

## Key takeaways

The EU AI Act's core obligations are enforceable now, not pending — August 2, 2026 already passed, fines reach up to 7% of global turnover, and national regulators can act on high-risk systems today. The Act's extraterritorial reach means plenty of non-EU businesses, especially software exporters and SaaS vendors with European customers, are in scope without realizing it. The realistic first move for most SMEs is not a legal overhaul — it is an honest inventory of what AI you actually use and an honest classification of its risk tier, which is the step almost everyone skips.
