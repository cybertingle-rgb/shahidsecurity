Current threat reporting is converging on an uncomfortable but useful reframe. Gartner's 2026-27 threat analysis names four specific categories — deepfakes, software supply chain risk, prompt injection, and AI application compromise — where, right now, the attacker holds the advantage over organizational defenses. Verizon's 2026 data shows vulnerability exploitation, not credential theft, is now the leading breach entry point, exploited faster than most patching cycles can keep up with. The honest conclusion from both: prevention, however well executed, is not a complete strategy on its own anymore.

## Cybersecurity vs cyber resilience

**Cybersecurity** reduces the probability that an attack succeeds — firewalls, patching, access control, security awareness training, all aimed at keeping attackers out or stopping them early.

**Cyber resilience** is the organization's ability to continue operating, or to recover quickly and predictably, when an attack succeeds anyway. It assumes prevention will, at some point, fail against a sufficiently capable or lucky attacker — and plans for that specific case rather than treating it as an edge condition.

These aren't competing priorities. Resilience doesn't replace prevention; it's what you build in addition to it, for the scenario prevention didn't catch.

## Why prevention is not enough on its own

This isn't a claim that prevention doesn't work — it's a claim that it doesn't work with the reliability needed to be the *only* layer. The structural reasons, drawn from current reporting: attackers are closing the gap between vulnerability disclosure and exploitation faster than most patching processes operate; AI-specific threat categories (prompt injection, deepfakes) are ones where tooling and organizational readiness genuinely haven't caught up yet; and third-party/supply-chain exposure means your security posture now partially depends on vendors whose patching timelines you don't control. None of these are solved by "doing prevention better" alone — they require an answer for what happens when prevention is bypassed anyway.

## The components of resilience

### Business continuity

Identifying which operations are genuinely critical, and how the business keeps functioning — even in a degraded state — while a security incident is being resolved rather than halting entirely.

### Incident response

A documented, specific plan: who's notified, in what order, who has authority to make which decisions, and what the first actions actually are — tested before it's needed, not written once and filed away.

### Backup strategy

Backups that are tested for actual restoration, not just confirmed to exist. A backup you've never tried restoring from is a hypothesis, not a plan — ransomware incidents specifically have exposed this gap repeatedly, where backups existed but restoration took far longer than the business could tolerate, or failed outright.

### Recovery

The practical sequence of getting systems back to operational state — which depends heavily on having a clear, pre-established priority order for what gets restored first, rather than deciding that under pressure during the incident itself.

### Crisis communication

A plan for communicating clearly and honestly with employees, customers, and (where relevant) regulators during an incident — the absence of this plan is consistently where organizations lose the most trust, independent of the technical severity of the incident itself.

### Identity recovery

Specifically worth planning for, given how much of current attack activity (see [Cloud Token Theft](/blog/cloud-token-theft/)) targets identity: how quickly can compromised credentials, tokens, and access grants actually be revoked and rotated across every system that trusted them?

### Critical systems and minimum viable operations

Defining, in advance, what "minimum viable operations" actually means for your specific business — which systems absolutely must function, even in a limited capacity, and which can reasonably stay offline during recovery without threatening the business itself.

## Recovery testing and tabletop exercises

Resilience plans that have never been exercised tend to reveal their gaps for the first time during a real incident — which is the most expensive possible time to discover them. A **tabletop exercise** — a structured walkthrough of a specific incident scenario with the actual people who'd be involved — surfaces unclear ownership, missing contact information, and untested assumptions cheaply, well before any of it matters for real.

## Cyber resilience checklist

- [ ] Identify your genuinely critical business operations and what "minimum viable" looks like for each
- [ ] Document an incident response plan with clear ownership and decision authority — not just a technical runbook
- [ ] Test backup restoration on a real schedule, not just confirm backups are being taken
- [ ] Build a crisis communication plan and template in advance, for employees, customers, and regulators as applicable
- [ ] Plan identity and access recovery specifically — how fast can compromised credentials and tokens be revoked everywhere they're trusted?
- [ ] Run a tabletop exercise at least annually, using a scenario realistic to your actual business and threat profile
- [ ] Revisit the plan after every real incident and every tabletop — treat both as inputs, not just the incident itself

## Key takeaways

Current threat reporting — attackers holding the advantage in several categories, vulnerability exploitation outpacing patch cycles — supports a specific, practical conclusion: prevention remains necessary but isn't sufficient on its own. Cyber resilience is the deliberate answer to "what happens when prevention doesn't work this time," built through tested backups, a real incident response plan, and recovery priorities decided in advance rather than under pressure.
