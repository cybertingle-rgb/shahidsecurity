Visiting family over a holiday, or sitting down with a less technical colleague after a quiet week, is the one reliable window most people get each year to actually fix the security gaps someone's been living with — not lecture about them, fix them. These nine take about twenty minutes combined and meaningfully change someone's actual risk, in order of how much they matter.

## 1. A password manager, and the end of password reuse

The single highest-impact fix on this list. If one reused password leaks in any breach — and reused passwords leak constantly, through breaches that have nothing to do with the account being attacked — every other account using that same password is exposed too. A password manager (built into the phone's OS, the browser, or a dedicated app) generates and remembers a unique password per site, which is the only realistic way a non-technical person stops reusing passwords without relying on memory.

## 2. Multi-factor authentication on the main email account specifically

Not every account — the one that matters most. Email is the recovery path for almost everything else: banking, social media, shopping accounts. Whoever controls someone's email can reset most of their other passwords. Turning on MFA for that one account, even if nothing else gets fixed today, closes the single biggest point of failure.

## 3. Passkeys where they're already offered

Google, Microsoft, and most major banks now support passkeys — a login method tied to the device itself (face, fingerprint, or PIN) rather than a password that can be phished or reused. Where it's already available, switching is a genuine upgrade with less day-to-day friction than a password, not more. Our [passkeys guide](/blog/passkeys-vs-passwords-2026/) covers the mechanics in more depth.

## 4. Actually installing the update, not dismissing the popup

The habit of tapping "remind me later" on every software update indefinitely is one of the most common gaps in non-technical device use. Security updates specifically patch the vulnerabilities attackers are actively exploiting — an unpatched device isn't equally safe for longer, it's accumulating known, exploitable gaps the whole time.

## 5. A real look at who still has access

Old shared logins (an ex-partner, a former roommate, a contractor from years ago), linked third-party apps nobody remembers approving, and devices logged into an account that haven't been seen in years are a common and rarely checked gap. A ten-minute review of "manage access" or "connected apps" settings on the main email and social accounts is worth doing once and then revisiting occasionally.

## 6. A backup that would actually survive a lost or broken phone

Years of photos and documents living only on one device is still common, and the loss is often discovered at the worst possible moment — a broken screen, a stolen phone, a failed update. Turning on automatic cloud backup (already built into most phones) takes a few minutes and removes the single most emotionally painful failure mode on this entire list.

## 7. The family verification phrase

AI voice-cloning has gotten good enough, and cheap enough to access, that a convincing fake emergency call "from" a family member asking for urgent money is a real, current scam pattern — not a hypothetical one. The [FTC's own guidance on family emergency scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/scams/family-emergency-scams) recommends agreeing on a private verification phrase in person, in advance, and treating any urgent money request — by call, text, or voice message — that can't produce it as a scam until proven otherwise. This takes one conversation and meaningfully closes a scam category that specifically targets the people who trust a familiar voice the most.

## 8. The "don't tap the delivery text" rule

A fake missed-delivery text claiming a small fee is owed is one of the most common and effective lures in active use, especially during high-shopping periods. The durable rule is simple enough for anyone to apply consistently: check delivery or account status only by opening the retailer's or carrier's own app or site directly, never through a link in an unexpected text.

## 9. Find My Device and a real lock screen, on every device

A lost or stolen device with no PIN, no biometric lock, and no "find my device" enabled turns a one-time inconvenience into a genuine account-takeover risk. All three take a few minutes to set up and are easy to forget precisely because they only matter on the one day something goes wrong.

## The twenty-minute checklist

- [ ] Password manager installed and set as the default for new accounts
- [ ] MFA enabled on the main email account
- [ ] Passkeys enabled wherever already offered (Google, Microsoft, major banks)
- [ ] Automatic software updates turned on, or a standing habit of installing them promptly
- [ ] A quick review of connected apps and shared logins on the main email/social accounts
- [ ] Automatic cloud backup enabled for photos and documents
- [ ] A private family verification phrase agreed on, in person
- [ ] Clear agreement to never act on a delivery/account text via its link — go direct instead
- [ ] Find My Device and a lock screen PIN/biometric enabled on every device

## Key takeaways

None of these nine fixes require technical skill to maintain once they're set up — the hard part is the first twenty minutes, not any ongoing effort afterward. For a business, the same list is close to the baseline most security awareness training is actually trying to instill in less technical employees; for a family, it's the single most useful thing a tech-comfortable relative can do with a short visit. Either way, the fixes that matter most — the password manager, email MFA, and the family codeword — are also the three people are most likely to keep putting off, precisely because nothing has gone wrong yet.
