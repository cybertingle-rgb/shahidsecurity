Ask most IT teams how many employees have system access, and they'll give you a confident number. Ask how many API keys, service accounts, and machine credentials have access to the same systems, and most won't know — and the real number is usually far larger than they'd guess. Multiple 2026 industry surveys put the ratio of machine identities to human employees well above 100:1 in typical organizations, and Gartner named identity and access management adapting to AI agents the #1 cybersecurity trend for 2026.

## What a non-human identity actually is

Non-human identities (NHIs) — sometimes called machine identities or workload identities — are credentials that authenticate on behalf of a system rather than a person:

- **API keys**, embedded in applications, scripts, and integrations
- **Service accounts**, used by applications to access databases, cloud storage, or other services
- **OAuth tokens and app-to-app connections**, especially for SaaS integrations
- **Workload identities**, for containers, microservices, and serverless functions
- **CI/CD pipeline credentials**, with access to deploy code and infrastructure
- **AI agent identities** — a newer and fast-growing category, since an autonomous AI agent acting on your systems needs its own credentials to do so

That last category is the one driving most of the recent growth. Industry surveys consistently point to AI and AI agent deployment as the primary reason machine identity counts have grown sharply over the past two years — every AI agent given access to a tool, a database, or an API needs its own credential, and organizations are deploying these agents faster than they're building processes to manage the identities behind them.

## Why non-human identities are a different kind of risk

A human employee account has a lifecycle most organizations actually manage: onboarding creates it, role changes update its permissions, offboarding disables it, and periodic access reviews catch what slips through. Non-human identities almost never get this treatment, for a few structural reasons:

- **They're created ad hoc**, often by a developer solving an immediate problem, with no equivalent to an HR offboarding trigger when the project ends.
- **They're over-provisioned by default.** Debugging a "permission denied" error in production under time pressure pushes most engineers toward granting broad access rather than narrowly scoped access — and nobody circles back to tighten it later.
- **They rarely expire.** Unlike a password an employee is prompted to rotate, a service account credential or API key often has no built-in expiration, and once working, nobody has a reason to touch it again — including after it should have been revoked.
- **Nobody owns the review.** Access reviews for employee accounts are a standard compliance exercise. Access reviews for the hundreds or thousands of machine credentials in a typical cloud environment usually aren't — because, in most organizations, no single process or owner exists for it.

The result: a credential created for a project that ended two years ago, with access nobody remembers granting, sitting unreviewed and unrevoked, is a common finding whenever an organization actually does a non-human identity audit for the first time. It's a nearly ideal target — it doesn't trigger login-anomaly alerts the way a compromised employee account would, since machine access patterns don't look unusual the way a 3am login from a new country does for a human user.

## The numbers, and why they vary so widely

Different 2026 surveys report different machine-to-human identity ratios — from roughly 45:1 up to several hundred to one — because each uses a different methodology and sample. But every credible source agrees on direction and scale:

- Gartner's 2025 Machine Identity survey found 94% of organizations seeing growth in machine identities, attributed mainly to AI and AI agent deployment.
- A 2026 industry survey of security leaders put the common machine-identity ratio above 100:1, with only about 12% of organizations reporting fully automated machine identity lifecycle management — meaning the overwhelming majority are managing this manually, inconsistently, or not at all.
- Gartner's own forecasting attributes a meaningful share of future enterprise breaches specifically to AI agent identity abuse, reflecting how directly this category is tied to the current wave of AI deployment.

## Practical first steps for managing non-human identity

You don't need a dedicated NHI management platform to make real progress. Start with what most organizations have never actually done:

- **Inventory what you have.** Pull a list of API keys, service accounts, and workload identities across your cloud environment and major SaaS platforms — most cloud providers and SaaS admin consoles can generate this list even without a dedicated tool.
- **Identify stale and unused credentials.** Anything unused for 90+ days is a strong candidate for revocation — the risk of breaking something is usually lower than people assume, and far lower than the risk of leaving it active indefinitely.
- **Scope new credentials narrowly from the start.** Default to least-privilege when creating a new service account or API key, rather than granting broad access "to be safe" and meaning to tighten it later — that follow-up rarely happens.
- **Set expiration where your tooling supports it.** Many cloud IAM systems support credential expiration or rotation policies that simply aren't turned on by default.
- **Assign ownership.** Every service account or API key should have a named owner responsible for knowing why it exists and reviewing whether it still should.
- **Apply the same discipline to AI agent credentials you're rolling out now**, rather than treating them as a special case outside your existing access-review process.

## Non-human identity security checklist

- [ ] Inventory API keys, service accounts, and workload identities across cloud and major SaaS platforms
- [ ] Identify and revoke credentials unused for 90+ days
- [ ] Assign a named owner to every service account and API key
- [ ] Default new credentials to least-privilege scope, not broad "just in case" access
- [ ] Enable credential expiration/rotation policies where your cloud IAM supports it
- [ ] Include AI agent credentials in the same access-review process as other machine identities
- [ ] Schedule a recurring (at least annual) non-human identity review — this category grows continuously
- [ ] Treat non-human identity as part of your incident response scope, not just human account compromise

## Key takeaways

Non-human identities now outnumber human employees by a wide margin in most organizations, and AI agent deployment is accelerating that gap further. Unlike employee accounts, machine credentials rarely get a lifecycle, rarely expire, and are almost never reviewed — which makes them a quiet, high-value target that doesn't trigger the anomaly detection built for human account compromise. The fix starts with an inventory most organizations have never done, not a new platform purchase.
