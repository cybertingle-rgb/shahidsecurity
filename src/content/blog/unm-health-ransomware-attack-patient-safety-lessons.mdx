A cyber incident at the **University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center (UNM HSC)** on October 26, 2026 forced the cancellation of surgeries and the diversion of ambulances from the hospital — **New Mexico's only Level 1 trauma center** — along with canceled appointments at more than 30 affiliated clinics. Systems were restored within roughly a day. A newly surfaced group calling itself "Byod" claimed responsibility on a leak site, but UNM itself has not characterized the incident as ransomware, and its own assessment describes something narrower than the attacker's claim. The gap between those two accounts is itself the most useful part of this story.

## What's confirmed, and what's still just a claim

**Confirmed, from UNM's own statements and multiple outlets:**
- The incident affected the entirety of UNM Health Sciences Center — the university's hospital, School of Medicine, College of Pharmacy, College of Nursing, and College of Population Health — plus UNM Sandoval Regional Medical Center, a separate 65-bed hospital.
- Surgeries were canceled, ambulances were diverted, and more than 30 clinics closed or canceled appointments during the disruption.
- All critical electronic systems and all UNM Health hospitals were confirmed fully operational again the following afternoon — a roughly one-day disruption window.
- UNM contacted state and federal law enforcement.

**Still a contested or unconfirmed claim:**
- A group calling itself "Byod" claimed the attack on its leak site. Coverage explicitly cautions that a ransomware group taking credit for an incident doesn't confirm they were actually responsible — claiming high-profile attacks for reputation is a known pattern independent of actual involvement.
- The claimed figure of **36,000 compromised machines** comes from the group's own leak-site listing, relayed by a third-party threat-intelligence source, and is unverified.
- **UNM's own assessment is narrower than the attacker's framing**: the university states the event affected some end-user devices but **did not penetrate core systems or servers** — a meaningfully different, less severe claim than "ransomware attack," and worth taking at face value rather than defaulting to the more dramatic version a leak-site claim implies.
- A separate report describes a breach affecting **600,000+ patients'** personal and health information at UNM Health. Whether that notice is connected to this same October 26 incident, a related one, or an entirely separate disclosure **isn't clearly established in available reporting** — we're flagging that gap rather than assuming the two are the same event.

## The real lesson isn't about who attacked, or even whether it was "really" ransomware

Strip away the attribution question entirely, and the confirmed facts alone are the useful part: **a roughly one-day IT disruption was serious enough to divert ambulances from a state's only Level 1 trauma center and cancel scheduled surgeries.** That's a fundamentally different category of impact than a typical business IT outage, and it's worth naming explicitly why: in most industries, a day of degraded IT systems costs money and productivity. In acute healthcare, the same disruption window can mean a trauma patient being routed to a facility that isn't the region's designated top-level trauma center — a safety consequence with no equivalent in most other sectors.

This connects directly to what we've covered in our guides to [ransomware readiness and backup/recovery planning](/blog/ransomware-readiness-backup-recovery-guide/) and [business continuity and disaster recovery](/blog/business-continuity-disaster-recovery-planning-guide/): a healthcare continuity plan has to account for **immediate, same-day failover for life-safety-critical systems specifically**, not just data recovery measured in days — because the data can often wait, but ambulance routing and surgical scheduling cannot.

## Security recommendations

- **If your organization provides any safety-critical or life-safety service, map which specific systems' downtime creates immediate safety consequences, separate from systems where downtime is merely costly.** A generic "restore within 72 hours" recovery target is inadequate for the first category, whatever it's adequate for in the second.
- **Treat an attacker's own claims about scope and severity with the same skepticism as any other unverified source** — UNM's own narrower assessment versus the attacker's framing is a clean illustration of why a leak-site claim shouldn't be taken as the final word on what actually happened.
- **Build a same-day emergency diversion and continuity plan specifically for clinical and life-safety systems**, tested as a real exercise, not just documented in a policy binder — the fact that UNM restored full operations within roughly a day suggests existing continuity planning functioned reasonably well here, which is itself worth highlighting as the harder, less-covered side of incident response: recovery speed, not just prevention.
- **Clarify your own breach notification and incident characterization process in advance**, so that if your organization is ever hit, your own assessment (not an attacker's claim or early media framing) becomes the account that reaches your patients, customers, or the public first.
- **Review cyber insurance coverage specifically for business interruption in safety-critical contexts**, as covered in our [cyber insurance requirements guide](/blog/cyber-insurance-requirements-2026/) — the cost model for a healthcare disruption that diverts ambulances is different from a standard data-breach cost model, and coverage should reflect that.

## Healthcare incident response checklist

- [ ] Identify which systems' downtime creates immediate patient-safety consequences, distinct from systems where downtime is costly but not dangerous
- [ ] Build and test a same-day emergency diversion/continuity plan specifically for life-safety-critical clinical systems
- [ ] Establish a rapid, accurate incident-characterization and public-communication process so your own assessment reaches stakeholders before unverified attacker claims do
- [ ] Review cyber insurance coverage for business-interruption costs specific to safety-critical service disruption
- [ ] Confirm breach-notification processes clearly distinguish confirmed facts from attacker claims in any public communication

## Key takeaways

The UNM Health incident is a genuine reminder that healthcare IT disruption carries a different severity class than the same disruption almost anywhere else — a roughly one-day outage was enough to divert ambulances from a state's only Level 1 trauma center and cancel scheduled surgeries. It's also a clean illustration of why attacker claims deserve scrutiny rather than automatic belief: UNM's own narrower assessment (affected end-user devices, not core systems) is a meaningfully different story than the "Byod" group's ransomware framing, and the responsible read takes neither claim at face value without the organization's own confirmation.
