CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog exists because of a simple, uncomfortable fact: the vulnerability that actually gets you breached is rarely the newest or most severe-sounding one — it's the one already being exploited against real targets, right now. That is the core value of threat intelligence, and it doesn't require a 24/7 security operations center to use. It requires knowing which few sources matter and checking them consistently.

## What threat intelligence actually is

Threat intelligence is information about active or emerging threats — specific enough to act on, not general awareness content. Useful threat intelligence answers questions like: which vulnerabilities are attackers actively exploiting this week, which indicators of compromise are tied to a current phishing or ransomware campaign, and which of your specific vendors or software have an active advisory out right now. This is different from general security news, which is written for broad awareness rather than operational action, and different from a vulnerability scan, which tells you what's present in your environment but not what's actively dangerous elsewhere.

## Free and low-cost sources worth actually using

A small business doesn't need a paid threat intelligence platform to get real value. A short, high-signal list is more sustainable than a sprawling one nobody checks:

- **[CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog](https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog)** — a continuously updated list of vulnerabilities CISA has confirmed are being actively exploited. If something you run appears here, it jumps to the top of your patching priority regardless of its CVSS score. Subscribe by email or RSS rather than checking manually.
- **[CISA Alerts and advisories](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories)** — current threat and vulnerability advisories, including joint advisories with international partners, covering specific active campaigns and software.
- **[StopRansomware.gov](https://www.stopransomware.gov/)** — CISA's dedicated ransomware resource, including advisories on specific ransomware groups and their current tactics.
- **Vendor security bulletins** for the specific software and cloud platforms your business actually runs — these are the most directly relevant advisories you'll receive, since they're scoped to your actual stack rather than the industry in general.
- **[MITRE ATT&CK](https://attack.mitre.org/)** — a knowledge base of real-world attacker tactics and techniques. Less a daily feed, more a reference for understanding how a described attack actually works and what it would look like in your own environment.
- **Your sector's ISAC (Information Sharing and Analysis Center)**, if one exists for your industry — groups like Health-ISAC or the Retail & Hospitality ISAC share threat intelligence specific to common targets and tactics in that sector, and several offer free or low-cost tiers for smaller organizations. Check eligibility and current terms directly with the relevant ISAC.
- **NIST's Small Business Cybersecurity Corner** — practical, small-business-scoped guidance rather than enterprise-oriented material, a useful complement to the more technical feeds above.

## A realistic workflow for a small team

- Subscribe to CISA KEV and relevant vendor bulletins by email or RSS rather than relying on remembering to check — the goal is the information coming to you, not you going looking for it.
- Set a short, recurring block of time (15-30 minutes, two or three times a week is a reasonable starting cadence) to review what came in.
- For each item, ask one question: does this affect something we actually run? Most won't — that's fine, and expected. The value is catching the ones that do, quickly.
- When something does apply, cross-reference it against your current patch status and asset inventory immediately, not at the next scheduled maintenance window if it's KEV-listed or clearly high-risk.
- Keep a simple log of what was reviewed and what action (if any) was taken — useful for your own accountability and, if you're ever asked, for demonstrating a real process to an auditor or cyber insurer.

## How this connects to vulnerability management and incident response

Threat intelligence is most valuable at the handoff points with other parts of your security program. It should directly feed your [patching priorities](/blog/vulnerability-prioritization-real-world-risk/) — a KEV listing is one of the strongest signals available for what to patch first, ahead of CVSS score alone. It should also inform incident response: knowing the current indicators and tactics associated with active campaigns targeting your sector or software means your team is more likely to recognize a real incident early, rather than dismissing unusual activity as noise until it's already caused damage.

## What to skip, at least at first

Automated indicator-sharing programs using formats like STIX/TAXII, and building out a dedicated threat intelligence platform, are genuinely useful at larger scale but represent real tooling and process overhead that most small businesses don't need to take on first. Get consistent value from the free, high-signal sources above before investing in anything more elaborate — the discipline of actually checking and acting on a short list matters more than the sophistication of the source list itself.

## Key takeaways

Threat intelligence for a small business doesn't require a SOC or a paid platform — it requires a short list of high-signal official sources (CISA's KEV catalog chief among them), a consistent review cadence, and a habit of cross-referencing anything relevant against your actual systems rather than treating it as general reading. Done consistently, even a lightweight version of this directly strengthens both patching priorities and incident detection.
