In April 2026, Grinex — a Kyrgyzstan-registered exchange already sanctioned by the US, UK, and EU for allegedly facilitating up to $6 billion in sanctions evasion — lost roughly $13.74 million to a hack and suspended all services two days later. The company blamed Western intelligence agencies; other analysts raised a false-flag question; nobody has independently confirmed attribution. What's not in dispute is the pattern underneath it: sanctioned exchanges keep getting breached, shut down, or reconstituted under new names, and the businesses that relied on them as a payment rail are left holding the risk.

Grinex wasn't an isolated case in 2026. The EU sanctioned HTX in July as part of an 18-company list tied to Russia-evasion tactics (HTX denies wrongdoing). In January, the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) designated the exchanges Zedcex and Zedxion for what it described as pivotal roles in a sanctions-evasion network benefiting the Iranian regime and the IRGC. And reporting has linked Bybit — the exchange behind the record 2025 hack attributed to North Korean actors — to roughly $2 billion in Iranian cash flow passing through its platform after the breach. The broader trend analysts describe: after one major sanctioned exchange (Garantex) was shut down in 2025, evasion activity simply migrated to the next platform, and enforcement has been playing catch-up ever since.

## Why this is a business risk, not just a geopolitics story

If your business accepts crypto payments, holds crypto on its balance sheet, or pays any vendor or contractor in crypto, the exchange or processor you rely on is a counterparty like any other vendor — except the due diligence most businesses apply to a new bank or payment processor rarely gets applied to a crypto rail. That gap matters more now for two concrete reasons:

1. **Exchange liability is being established in court.** A 2026 US court ruling held that crypto exchanges can be held liable for sanctions evasion occurring on their own platforms — not just the individual users doing the evading. That raises the bar for exchanges to actually screen their own flows, but it also means a business relying on an exchange that later turns out to have been facilitating evasion is now dealing with a counterparty under direct regulatory and legal exposure, not a neutral utility.
2. **Sanctioned platforms don't announce themselves.** Grinex, Zedcex, and Zedxion were not household names before their designations. A newly launched exchange with clean branding, a plausible jurisdiction, and competitive fees can be — knowingly or not — a continuation of a previously sanctioned operation's infrastructure. Screening by company name alone misses this; screening needs to reach the wallet and settlement layer.

## What to actually check

For any business that accepts, holds, or pays out in crypto, this is a practical due-diligence list, not a theoretical one:

- **Screen settlement counterparties, not just your direct processor.** Ask your payment processor explicitly which exchanges and liquidity providers they settle through, and whether that list has changed in the last 12 months.
- **Screen at the wallet-address level**, against OFAC's SDN list and equivalent EU/UK lists, for any crypto addresses your business transacts with directly — not just company names, which change faster than addresses do.
- **Treat a sudden platform shutdown or rebrand as a signal**, not just an inconvenience. Grinex's collapse happened within 48 hours of a hack; businesses with funds or pending settlements on a platform that disappears that fast have very little recourse.
- **Document the screening you do.** If a regulator or bank later asks why your business was exposed to a sanctioned platform, "we didn't know" is a weaker position than "here is our screening process and when we last ran it."
- **Build this into existing vendor risk review**, the same way you'd review a new bank or processor — see our broader guide to [third-party vendor risk management](/blog/third-party-vendor-risk-management-smb/) for the underlying discipline this fits into.

## The pattern to watch going forward

Blockchain analytics firm Elliptic has said it expects increased sanctions enforcement actions against crypto platforms through the rest of 2026, and the Grinex-to-HTX-to-Zedcex sequence suggests enforcement is accelerating, not slowing. For a business, the practical takeaway isn't to avoid crypto payment rails entirely — it's to treat the exchange or processor you rely on with the same counterparty scrutiny you'd apply to any other financial infrastructure your business depends on, because regulators increasingly do.
