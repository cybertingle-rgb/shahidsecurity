Three separate regulatory stories from 2026 point at the same underlying shift, even though none of them were framed that way individually: weak cybersecurity is no longer treated by regulators as primarily a company's own problem. It's increasingly treated as a financial-crime enabler — the thing that lets sanctions evasion, laundering, and fraud reach a scale that wouldn't otherwise be possible.

## The evidence, from three different angles

**Exchange hacks are financial-crime events, not just breaches.** When a sanctioned exchange like Grinex gets hacked and immediately shuts down, or when reporting links a major 2025 exchange breach to roughly $2 billion in Iranian cash flow passing through the platform afterward, the hack itself isn't the end of the story — it's the beginning of a laundering and sanctions-evasion chain that a more secure platform wouldn't have enabled at that scale. A 2026 US court ruling made this connection explicit by holding that exchanges can be held liable for sanctions evasion occurring on their own platforms — the platform's security and controls are now part of its legal exposure, not separate from it. See our [analysis of sanctioned exchanges](/blog/sanctioned-crypto-exchanges-business-counterparty-risk/) for the specific 2026 cases.

**Stablecoin stability is explicitly framed as a security question.** The IMF, FSB, and BIS have all flagged, in various ways, that a stablecoin issuer's operational resilience — including its security against exploits — is directly tied to systemic financial stability, because a major security failure could trigger the exact run-and-contagion scenario regulators are trying to prevent. That's a significant reframing: a security incident at a stablecoin issuer isn't just that company's breach, it's a potential financial-stability event. Our [piece on stablecoin regulation](/blog/stablecoin-regulation-security-baseline-2026/) covers this in more depth.

**National AML/CFT regimes are building security requirements directly into licensing.** Pakistan's new Virtual Assets Act — built explicitly in response to ongoing FATF scrutiny — requires licensed virtual asset providers to maintain transaction monitoring, sanctions screening, and Travel Rule compliance as conditions of operating at all, not as optional best practice. See our [breakdown of Pakistan's PVARA regime](/blog/pakistan-virtual-assets-act-pvara-fatf-warning-2026/) for the specifics. This mirrors a broader pattern already established in frameworks like the EU's DORA, which requires financial entities to formally manage the ICT security of every vendor in their chain — see our [DORA compliance guide](/blog/dora-compliance-ict-third-party-risk-guide/) for how that flows down to vendors that aren't financial companies themselves.

## Why this matters even if you're not a crypto company

The specific examples above are mostly crypto-related because that's where 2026's enforcement activity concentrated, but the underlying logic isn't crypto-specific. Any business that sits anywhere near a payment chain, holds customer funds even briefly, or depends on a banking relationship is increasingly subject to the same reframing: your security posture isn't just about whether you get breached — it's about whether your systems could be, or already have been, a channel for financial crime that regulators, banks, and insurers now expect you to actively prevent, not just passively avoid causing.

Cyber insurers have moved the same direction — our guide to [2026 cyber insurance requirements](/blog/cyber-insurance-requirements-2026/) covers how insurers increasingly expect demonstrable security controls before they'll cover a business at all, which is the same underlying shift from a different angle.

## The practical response

For most businesses, the useful response isn't a dramatic overhaul — it's recognizing that security review and compliance review, which are often run as separate exercises by separate teams or vendors, are now the same conversation as far as regulators are concerned. A security gap is a compliance gap. A compliance gap often traces back to a security gap. Reviewing them together, against whichever regulatory frameworks actually apply to your business, is both more accurate than treating them separately and generally cheaper than fixing the same underlying problem twice under two different initiatives.
