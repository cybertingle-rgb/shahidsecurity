Shadow IT — employees adopting software the IT department never approved — has been a known risk for years. Shadow AI is the same pattern, moving much faster. In the space of about twelve months, the share of employees using unauthorized AI tools on corporate devices roughly tripled, rising from around 15% to around 45% of the workforce, according to Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report — making it the third most common source of non-malicious data loss the report tracks.

## What is shadow AI?

Shadow AI is any AI tool — a public chatbot, a browser-based writing assistant, a coding copilot, an image generator — that an employee is using for work without the organization having approved, reviewed, or in many cases even noticed it. It covers two overlapping patterns:

- **Unsanctioned use of sanctioned-category tools.** The company hasn't approved any AI chatbot, but employees use one anyway because it makes their job faster.
- **Unsanctioned use through personal accounts.** Even where a company has an approved AI tool, employees often access AI services through personal, non-corporate logins instead — which means none of the organization's data controls, retention policies, or audit logging apply to what gets pasted in.

Verizon's 2026 DBIR data on this is specific: of employees using unauthorized AI tools, roughly two-thirds are doing so through personal accounts rather than any company-provisioned access. That's the real exposure — not that AI is being used, but that it's being used completely outside any visibility the organization has.

## Why employees use unapproved AI tools

Almost never out of malice. The honest reasons are mundane and relatable:

- The approved tool (if one exists) is slower, more restricted, or simply worse than the free consumer version.
- There's no approved tool at all, and waiting for IT to evaluate and roll one out takes months the employee doesn't have.
- The employee doesn't think of it as a security decision — pasting a paragraph into a chatbot feels the same as using a spell-checker, not like transmitting company data to a third party.

That last point matters most for how you respond to this. A punitive "we caught you" approach treats a visibility gap as a discipline problem, which tends to push the behavior further underground rather than fixing it.

## What information can leak

Verizon's 2026 report analyzed over 850,000 data-loss-prevention events specifically involving generative AI tools. The breakdown of what employees are submitting is worth sitting with:

- **Source code** was the single most common data type submitted to external AI models — ahead of structured business data, images, or research documents.
- **Proprietary documents and research** — strategy documents, internal reports, draft contracts.
- **Customer and business data** pasted in for "quick summaries" or "make this clearer" requests.

None of this requires an attacker. It's an employee, trying to do their job faster, sending company data to a system outside the company's control — where retention, training-data use, and third-party access depend entirely on that AI provider's terms of service, which the employee almost certainly never read.

## Shadow AI vs shadow IT: what's different

The underlying pattern — unauthorized software, invisible to IT — is identical to classic shadow IT. Two things make shadow AI a sharper version of the same problem:

1. **The interface invites disclosure.** A spreadsheet tool doesn't ask you "what's your biggest business problem right now?" A chatbot does, by design, and people answer honestly.
2. **The data doesn't stay local.** Shadow IT historically meant an unapproved app running on a device you still controlled. Shadow AI means the data has already left — it's on a third party's infrastructure the moment it's submitted, regardless of what happens next.

## How attackers can exploit AI data exposure

Shadow AI itself usually isn't an attack — it's an exposure that creates downstream risk:

- **Aggregated data leakage.** Enough fragments (a product roadmap here, a customer list there, source code snippets elsewhere) submitted across many employees and many sessions can reconstruct a picture no single document would have revealed.
- **Prompt and output interception.** Depending on the tool and account type, inputs and outputs may be logged, retained, or used for model training in ways the organization never agreed to and can't audit.
- **Credential and secret exposure.** Developers pasting code to debug it routinely paste API keys, connection strings, and tokens along with it, because they're focused on the bug, not on what else is in the snippet.
- **A new target for attackers directly.** If an organization's shadow AI usage pattern is predictable (which tools, roughly how), that pattern itself becomes something a targeted attacker can map and exploit — knowing where unmonitored data flows exist is valuable reconnaissance.

## How to detect shadow AI in your organization

You can't fix what you can't see. Realistic starting points, roughly in order of effort:

- **DNS and web-proxy logs.** Even without a dedicated tool, reviewing outbound traffic to known AI-service domains over a sample period gives a rough usage baseline.
- **CASB / SSE tooling**, if already deployed for other SaaS visibility, usually already has AI-service categorization built in or easily added.
- **A short, honest internal survey.** Asking "what AI tools do you currently use for work, approved or not?" with an explicit no-consequences framing for the first round gets surprisingly candid answers and is far cheaper than any tooling rollout.
- **DLP policies tuned for AI-service destinations specifically**, not just generic "cloud upload" categories, since AI chat interfaces don't look like a traditional file upload.

## Building an AI usage policy that actually gets followed

A policy that's purely restrictive ("AI tools are forbidden") tends to fail the same way blanket shadow-IT bans failed: it doesn't remove the underlying pressure to use the tool, it just removes the organization's visibility into how. A policy more likely to hold:

- **Name an approved tool or tools**, even a modest one, so there's a legitimate path that's actually faster than going around policy.
- **Be specific about data categories**, not just "don't leak sensitive data" — list the concrete things employees actually handle (customer PII, source code, unreleased financials, contracts) that must never go into a non-approved AI tool.
- **Explain the why, briefly**, rather than issuing the rule bare. "We don't control what happens to data once it's submitted to a service we haven't reviewed" is a sentence most employees will actually internalize.
- **Pair the policy with the technical controls below** — a policy nobody can technically violate accidentally is more durable than one relying purely on employees remembering it.

## Technical controls worth prioritizing

- **DLP (Data Loss Prevention)** tuned for AI-service destinations, as above — this is the single highest-leverage control, since it catches the behavior regardless of intent.
- **CASB / SSE (Cloud Access Security Broker / Security Service Edge)** to apply consistent policy across both sanctioned and emerging AI-service categories, including newly-launched tools a static blocklist would miss.
- **Identity controls**: where an approved AI tool exists, require SSO through it rather than allowing ad-hoc account creation — this alone closes the "personal account" visibility gap for a large share of use.
- **Logging and retention policy on the approved tool**, reviewed the same way you'd review any other vendor handling company data.
- **A lightweight AI governance owner** — not necessarily a new hire, but a named person or small group responsible for evaluating new AI tool requests on a reasonable timeline, so "there's no approved option" stops being the default excuse for going around policy.

## Shadow AI security checklist

- [ ] Baseline current AI tool usage (DNS/proxy logs, CASB data, or an honest internal survey)
- [ ] Identify and approve at least one capable AI tool per major use case (writing, coding, research)
- [ ] Require SSO/corporate login for any approved AI tool — no personal-account access to company data
- [ ] Write a specific, short AI usage policy naming concrete data categories, not vague principles
- [ ] Deploy or tune DLP for AI-service destinations specifically
- [ ] Add AI-service categories to CASB/SSE policy if already deployed for other SaaS
- [ ] Review the approved tool's own data retention and training-use terms before approving it
- [ ] Name an owner for evaluating new AI tool requests, with a real turnaround commitment
- [ ] Revisit the policy and tooling every two quarters — this category moves faster than most

## Key takeaways

Shadow AI isn't a hypothetical risk category — it roughly tripled in a single year and is already a top-three source of non-malicious data loss, per Verizon's 2026 DBIR. The fix isn't primarily technical and it isn't primarily punitive: it's giving employees a legitimate, fast, reasonably capable approved option, backed by DLP and CASB controls that catch the gap for everyone who doesn't wait for one.
