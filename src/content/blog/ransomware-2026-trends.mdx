Ransomware headlines tend to focus on the ransom amount or the company name. The more operationally important story in 2026 is about speed and stealth: how fast attackers move once they're in, and how much of that movement is designed to look completely normal until it's too late.

## The numbers, from CrowdStrike's 2026 Global Threat Report

CrowdStrike's 2026 Global Threat Report gives some of the clearest current data on how ransomware and broader eCrime operations actually behave:

- **Average breakout time — the time from initial access to broader lateral movement — dropped to roughly 29 minutes.** The fastest recorded instance was just **27 seconds**.
- **82% of detections in 2025 were "malware-free."** Rather than deploying obviously malicious software that antivirus tools are built to catch, attackers increasingly use legitimate administrative tools and valid, often stolen, credentials already present in the environment.
- **AI-enabled attacks rose 89%** year over year, reflecting broader adoption of AI tooling by attackers — from automating credential theft to nation-state actors deploying LLM-powered malware.
- **Cloud-conscious intrusions — attacks specifically designed around cloud environments — rose 37% overall, and 266% among state-nexus (nation-state-linked) actors.** Ransomware groups are increasingly comfortable operating across cloud, identity, and virtualization layers, not just traditional on-premise networks.
- **Supply chain compromise was a defining tactic of 2025**, with adversaries targeting upstream software providers and public code repositories to gain broad, indirect access to many downstream organisations at once (see our related article on [software supply chain attacks](/blog/software-supply-chain-attacks-2026/)).

## Two emerging shifts worth knowing about specifically

**Encryptionless extortion.** As ransom payment rates have declined — more organisations either refuse to pay or have recovery capability that removes the pressure to pay — some groups have shifted tactics entirely. Rather than encrypting data and demanding payment for the decryption key, they steal the data and threaten to leak it, skipping encryption altogether. The business impact (extortion, reputational risk, regulatory exposure) remains, even though the technical signature looks different from classic ransomware.

**Post-quantum cryptography in new ransomware families.** Some newly observed ransomware strains have begun adopting post-quantum-resistant encryption ciphers — a forward-looking move by attackers that has no near-term practical effect on victims today, but signals that ransomware tooling is being actively developed and hardened, not stagnating.

## Why "malware-free" and "fast" together are the real problem

The combination of these two trends is what makes modern ransomware genuinely harder to defend against than the ransomware of a few years ago. A malware-free intrusion using legitimate tools and valid credentials doesn't trigger the same alarms a piece of custom malware would. Combined with a breakout time measured in minutes, the practical window for a human security analyst to notice something's wrong, investigate, and contain it before it spreads has compressed dramatically. This isn't a reason for panic — it's a reason to make sure detection and response capability, not just prevention, is part of your security posture.

## Security recommendations

- **Assume prevention will occasionally fail, and invest in detection accordingly.** Monitoring for unusual use of legitimate admin tools (not just malware signatures) is now a baseline requirement, not an advanced capability.
- **Maintain and actually test backups on a 3-2-1 basis** (three copies, two different media, one off-site or offline) — the single factor that most changes your options during an active incident.
- **Segment identity and cloud access** so that a compromised credential in one system doesn't provide a fast path to everything else, especially given the sharp rise in cloud-conscious intrusions.
- **Have an incident response plan written down before you need it.** Deciding roles, communication steps, and escalation paths during an active incident costs time you don't have; deciding them in advance is free.
- **Review third-party and supply chain access regularly** given how often 2025's major incidents traced back to a trusted upstream provider rather than a direct attack.

## Key takeaways

The defining shift in ransomware behaviour in 2026 isn't a new kind of malware — it's speed and camouflage. Breakout times measured in minutes, combined with attackers increasingly using legitimate tools and credentials rather than obvious malware, mean that detection and tested recovery capability matter as much as prevention. Businesses with a tested backup strategy and a written incident response plan aren't just recovering faster when something happens; they're negotiating from a position where paying a ransom is a choice, not a necessity.
