Reuters confirmed on March 27, 2026 that two Chinese universities with documented military research ties — Beihang University and Harbin Institute of Technology — bought Super Micro Computer server systems configured with Nvidia A100 AI chips, despite both institutions sitting on the US Entity List, which is supposed to make it difficult for American companies to legally supply them. Separately, and this is where headlines have started blurring two different stories together, a Bloomberg-sourced report says seven Chinese military-linked universities are now seeking access to Nvidia's newer H200 chips. That second claim describes an active pursuit, not a confirmed purchase — and getting the distinction right matters, because the real, confirmed story is itself a solid lesson in supply-chain and export-control risk without needing the unconfirmed part to make the point.

## What's actually confirmed

- A March 16, 2026 procurement notice showed **Beihang University** — one of China's "Seven Sons of National Defence," with documented work on missile, satellite, and robotics technology — bought a machine-learning workstation built on a Super Micro system configured with four Nvidia A100 chips.
- A July 2026 tender showed **Harbin Institute of Technology**, another of the "Seven Sons," bought a Super Micro server with eight Nvidia A100 chips.
- Both universities are on the **US Entity List**, a US Commerce Department blacklist intended to require a specific export license — rarely granted — before American companies can supply them with controlled technology, including these chips.
- Neither university responded to Reuters' requests for comment. Super Micro declined to comment on the documents' contents. Nvidia said it "continues to work closely with customers and the US government on compliance" as export regulations have expanded.
- **It's genuinely unclear, even to Reuters' reporting, exactly how the universities were able to purchase these systems** — the open question at the center of the story.
- This reporting came days after three people connected to Super Micro, including a co-founder, were charged in a separate case alleging they helped smuggle at least **$2.5 billion** worth of US AI technology into China.

## What's still an unconfirmed, separate claim

A different, Bloomberg-sourced report says **seven Chinese military-linked universities are actively seeking access to Nvidia H200 chips** — a newer, more powerful part than the A100s in the confirmed Reuters story. Reuters' own reporting separately says Nvidia secured **Beijing's approval** to sell the H200 into the Chinese market — but Chinese government approval to sell is not the same as a US export license to sell to a blacklisted end user, and potential buyers still need their own federal export authorization before any H200 chips can legally change hands. **No confirmed H200 sale to a blacklisted institution has been reported as of this writing.** Treat the "seeking H200 chips" detail as a developing, unconfirmed thread — distinct from the already-confirmed A100 story — not as the same event with a bigger number attached.

This is the second Nvidia-and-China story to break in as many weeks, after the [Nvidia CEO's personal relationship with a Chinese businesswoman intelligence sources flagged as a potential espionage vector](/blog/nvidia-ceo-china-spy-scandal-business-lessons/). They are unrelated events involving different people and different claims — worth naming explicitly so the two don't get conflated into one bigger, less accurate story than either actually is.

## The real supply-chain lesson, independent of the unresolved parts

Strip away the specific chips and universities, and the confirmed story illustrates a risk pattern that shows up in far more ordinary supply chains than advanced AI hardware:

- **A blacklist or sanctions list is only as good as the due diligence applied before a sale, not after.** Once a restricted system reaches an entity it was never supposed to reach, there's no technical control inside the hardware that can retroactively enforce the export rule — the control point was always the transaction, and in this case, Reuters itself couldn't determine how that control point was bypassed.
- **A reseller or distributor relationship is a real compliance surface, not just a sales channel.** Whether the failure here was a knowing evasion, a shell-entity purchase, or simple negligence further down a distribution chain is unresolved — and for a smaller business selling through resellers or distributors into any regulated or sanctioned market, that same ambiguity is exactly the gap a compliance program needs to close before a sale, not explain after one.
- **End-use and end-user verification is a distinct discipline from checking a company's name against a published list.** A sanctioned entity doesn't have to buy directly — a document-based procurement process, an intermediate buyer, or a reseller with incomplete visibility into its own customer's customer can all produce the same outcome: restricted technology reaching a restricted end user without anyone in the chain making an obviously wrong decision at any single step.

## Security and compliance recommendations

- **If your business sells, licenses, or distributes technology — hardware, software, or data services — into markets with export-control exposure, build an end-use and end-user verification step into the sale itself**, not as a one-time check against a sanctions list but as a standing part of onboarding any reseller or distributor relationship.
- **Treat your distributor and reseller network as a compliance surface you're responsible for assessing, not a boundary where your due diligence obligation ends.** A distributor's own customer vetting practices are worth reviewing before relying on them, not after a restricted sale surfaces in the press.
- **Don't assume a government-level trade approval (like Nvidia's reported Beijing approval to sell H200 chips) substitutes for your own required export authorization.** Two different governments' approval processes are not interchangeable, and conflating them is exactly the kind of mistake that turns an administrative gap into a real violation.
- **If your compliance program only checks a counterparty's name against a sanctions list at onboarding, add a periodic re-check.** Entity List status, sanctions designations, and ownership structures all change — a clean check at the start of a relationship doesn't stay clean indefinitely.
- **Document the reasoning behind every export-sensitive sale decision, not just the outcome.** If a transaction is ever questioned, having a recorded basis for why it was approved is the difference between a defensible compliance program and an exposed one.

## Export-control and third-party risk checklist

- [ ] Confirm whether your business's products or services have any export-control classification requiring end-use/end-user screening
- [ ] Review whether reseller and distributor relationships include a standing obligation to verify their own customers' eligibility
- [ ] Add a periodic re-screening step for existing customers and partners against current sanctions and entity lists, not just a one-time check at onboarding
- [ ] Document the compliance reasoning behind export-sensitive sales decisions as they're made, not reconstructed after the fact
- [ ] Clarify internally that a counterparty government's approval to buy is not a substitute for your own required export authorization to sell

## Key takeaways

The confirmed facts are serious enough on their own: two blacklisted, military-linked Chinese universities obtained export-controlled Nvidia chips through a still-unexplained purchase path, in a case that broke days after Super Micro executives were charged with smuggling billions of dollars of the same category of technology. The unconfirmed H200-seeking claim is worth watching, not worth repeating as settled fact. For any business with export-sensitive products, resellers, or international customers, the actual lesson doesn't depend on how the unresolved part of this story turns out — it's that a sanctions list is a starting point for due diligence, not a substitute for it.
