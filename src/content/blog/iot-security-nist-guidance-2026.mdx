Connected devices — cameras, sensors, access control systems, building automation — have quietly become one of the largest and least-monitored parts of most organisations' networks. Two regulatory developments in 2026 are pushing IoT security back onto the agenda: an update to NIST's core device guidance, and new EU reporting obligations that start affecting the market this month.

## NIST's guidance update

NIST has opened a revised draft of its IoT Product Cybersecurity Guidance — **[SP 800-213 Revision 1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-213.pdf)** — for public review, with comments accepted through August 24, 2026. The update strengthens risk management expectations for connected devices used in federal environments, and its core requirements are a genuinely useful checklist for any organisation deploying IoT devices, not just government agencies:

- **Secure coding and vulnerability testing** during development, not bolted on afterward.
- **Secure mechanisms for software and firmware updates** — a device that can't be patched safely and reliably is a liability from day one, regardless of how it was built.
- **Appropriate encryption and integrity verification** to protect data confidentiality and integrity in transit and at rest.
- **Logging, audit, and event monitoring support**, so that a compromise can actually be detected rather than persisting silently — a capability many consumer-grade and even some commercial IoT devices still lack entirely.

## Why this matters right now: scale and the EU deadline

The scale of the underlying problem is significant. Over **21,500 CVEs were disclosed in the first half of 2026 alone** across the software and hardware landscape, and IoT firmware is a disproportionately large and under-patched share of that. Compromised cameras and routers were behind some of the largest recorded DDoS attacks of the past two years, with volumes approaching **30 Tbps** — driven largely by devices running outdated firmware with no realistic update path.

On the regulatory side, the EU's Cyber Resilience Act introduces mandatory reporting obligations for products with digital elements sold in the EU market, with those reporting obligations taking effect **September 11, 2026** — this month — and the fuller set of compliance obligations following in December 2027. Any business selling, integrating, or deploying connected devices with EU market exposure has a genuinely near-term deadline to understand.

## What this looks like in practice: a real example

This isn't abstract for us. Shahid Security's founder's own independent research — [Threats & Attacks on an IoT-Based Smart Campus System](/case-studies/iot-smart-campus-threat-analysis/) — modelled exactly this problem in a controlled academic environment: a smart campus network connecting parking, library access, ID cards, attendance, and exam systems onto shared infrastructure. The research demonstrated a full attack chain — ARP spoofing, traffic interception, DNS spoofing, and downstream script injection — starting from a single compromised device and pivoting into the wider network segment. The root cause wasn't a flaw in any individual sensor or the cloud backend; it was **unauthenticated ARP and DNS resolution** at the network layer, the same category of foundational weakness NIST's guidance is aimed at preventing.

That finding generalises well beyond a university campus: any environment mixing IoT devices with sensitive systems on a flat network — a medical facility, an office building's access control, a retail chain's point-of-sale sensors — carries the same structural risk if devices aren't authenticated and segmented properly.

## Security recommendations

- **Segment IoT devices onto their own network**, separate from the systems holding sensitive data, so a single compromised device isn't a direct path into everything else.
- **Confirm a realistic firmware update path exists before deploying a device**, not after. A device that can't be patched is accumulating risk from the day it's installed.
- **Enable device-level logging where it's supported**, and make sure something actually reviews it — visibility without monitoring provides no real protection.
- **Check for basic network-layer authentication** (address resolution and DNS integrity, not just device passwords) — this was the actual root cause in our own IoT research, not the devices themselves.
- **If you sell or integrate connected products into the EU market, review the Cyber Resilience Act's reporting requirements now**, given the September 2026 obligations are already in effect.

## Key takeaways

IoT security in 2026 is being pushed forward by two forces at once: an updated NIST framework raising the bar on what "secure by design" actually requires, and EU regulation imposing real reporting deadlines starting this month. Underneath both is a consistent technical reality, one we've demonstrated directly in our own independent research: the weakest point in a connected-device environment is often not the device itself, but the unauthenticated network layer it sits on.
