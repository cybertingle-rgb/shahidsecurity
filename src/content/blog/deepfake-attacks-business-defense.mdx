Gartner's 2026-27 threat analysis names deepfakes among four critical categories where the attacker currently holds the advantage — not because deepfakes are new, but because they've matured to the point of reliably evading casual detection, while the organizational habits needed to catch them haven't kept pace.

## What is a deepfake attack?

A deepfake attack uses AI-generated audio, video, or both to impersonate a real person convincingly enough to manipulate a target into taking an action they wouldn't take for a stranger — approving a payment, sharing credentials, or granting access, typically under time pressure.

### Voice cloning

Modern voice-cloning tools can produce a convincing replica of someone's voice from a relatively small sample of real audio — increasingly, public sources like earnings calls, conference talks, or social media videos are enough. The resulting clip can be used live (in a phone call) or pre-recorded (in a voicemail or voice message).

### Video deepfakes

Video deepfakes range from pre-recorded manipulated footage to, increasingly, real-time video call impersonation — a fabricated likeness responding live, in a video meeting, to questions asked of it.

## Executive impersonation

The most common business-targeting pattern is executive impersonation: a deepfaked CEO, CFO, or other senior figure instructing an employee — almost always in finance, HR, or IT — to take an urgent action. The attack leans on two things that have worked in social engineering for decades: authority and urgency. The AI generation is what's new; the psychological lever is old.

### Fake video calls and fake voice calls

Both patterns follow the same structure: an unexpected, time-pressured request, arriving through a channel that appears legitimate (a video call that looks like the CEO, a voicemail that sounds like them), asking for something that bypasses normal process "just this once, because I'm traveling / in a meeting / unreachable right now."

## What attackers actually go after

- **Payment fraud.** The classic target — an urgent wire transfer or payment change, dressed up with a plausible business reason and deepfaked authority to override the employee's normal hesitation.
- **Credential theft.** A deepfaked IT or security team member requesting a password, MFA code, or remote access "to fix an urgent issue."

## How attackers build trust before the ask

The deepfake itself is usually the final step, not the first. A convincing attack typically includes groundwork — reconnaissance on the organization's structure and reporting lines (often from public sources: LinkedIn, press releases, earnings calls), sometimes an earlier, lower-stakes contact to establish a pattern of legitimate-seeming communication before the real request arrives.

## How employees can verify identity

- **Out-of-band verification.** Confirm any unusual or high-value request through a separate communication channel — call back on a known number, not one provided in the suspicious message itself.
- **MFA on the approval action, not just account login.** A second factor specifically tied to high-value actions (payment approval, access grants) adds a step a deepfake alone can't talk its way past.
- **Approval workflows that don't bend for urgency.** The single most effective structural defense: a payment or access-change process that requires a second approver, with no "skip this because it's urgent" exception — because urgency is the exact lever these attacks rely on.

## Deepfake detection limitations

Detection technology is real and improving, but treating it as a complete defense is a mistake worth naming directly: generation quality and detection capability are in a genuine arms race, and an organization that depends entirely on "we'll be able to tell it's fake" is betting on staying ahead of that race indefinitely. Process controls that don't require spotting the fake in the moment — out-of-band verification, mandatory second approval — remain effective even against a deepfake good enough to fool detection tooling entirely.

## Business security checklist

- [ ] Establish out-of-band verification as standard practice for any payment, access, or credential request that arrives via call, video, or message — no exceptions for seniority or urgency
- [ ] Require a second approver for payment changes and high-value transfers, with no urgency-based override
- [ ] Train finance, HR, and IT staff specifically — they're the highest-value targets for executive impersonation
- [ ] Reduce public exposure of information useful for impersonation prep (detailed org charts, travel schedules) where reasonably possible
- [ ] Add MFA specifically to high-value approval actions, not just login
- [ ] Treat an unexpected urgent request as a signal to slow down, not speed up, regardless of who it appears to be from

## Key takeaways

Deepfake attacks succeed by combining AI-generated impersonation with decades-old social engineering pressure — authority plus urgency. Detection technology helps but isn't a complete answer, since generation quality keeps improving alongside it. The durable defense is procedural: out-of-band verification and mandatory second approval for anything high-stakes, applied without exception even when the request appears to come from the top of the organization.
