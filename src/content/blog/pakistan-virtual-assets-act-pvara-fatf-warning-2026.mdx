Pakistan's relationship with cryptocurrency has swung sharply in 2026 — and the direction of that swing is a direct response to a warning FATF has been issuing consistently for years. Understanding what changed, and why, matters for any Pakistani business that touches virtual assets, and for any international business dealing with Pakistani counterparties who might.

## What actually changed in 2026

For seven years, Pakistan maintained an effective ban on banks servicing cryptocurrency businesses. That changed in April 2026, when the State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) issued a circular allowing regulated banks to open accounts for virtual asset service providers (VASPs) — but only those licensed under the new regime, and banks themselves still cannot trade, invest in, or hold digital assets with their own funds or customer deposits.

The regime that made this possible is the **Virtual Assets Act, 2026**, which formally established the **Pakistan Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (PVARA)** as the independent federal regulator for the sector. PVARA's licensing perimeter is broad: exchanges, custodians, wallet providers, token issuers, and investment platforms serving users in Pakistan all fall within it, regardless of where the business is incorporated — branch offices are explicitly reported as insufficient; local incorporation is required. Capital thresholds reportedly range from PKR 100 million to PKR 1 billion depending on the activity, and directors and key personnel face fit-and-proper checks before licensing.

## The FATF warning underneath all of it

Pakistan exited the FATF grey list in October 2022, after years of enhanced monitoring over money-laundering and terror-financing deficiencies. But FATF's own messaging since then has been consistent and explicit: **grey-list removal does not mean a country is immune to money-laundering or terror-financing risk** — it means the specific deficiencies identified at the time were addressed. Pakistan has remained under FATF follow-up monitoring since its exit.

The AML/CFT provisions built into the Virtual Assets Act are a direct response to that ongoing scrutiny. Reported requirements include mandatory KYC, transaction monitoring, recordkeeping, sanctions screening, and suspicious activity reporting for all licensed VASPs, with explicit alignment to **FATF Recommendations 15 and 16** — the standards covering virtual assets and the "Travel Rule," which requires originator and beneficiary information to accompany crypto transfers above a threshold. Separately, VASPs are reported to face obligations under Pakistan's Financial Monitoring Unit (FMU) goAML system. As of late October 2026, government reporting indicated plans to tighten these rules further specifically to meet FATF guidelines — including giving PVARA expanded customer due-diligence powers.

This is the warning, in plain terms: Pakistan's crypto sector is being built, from the ground up, around the assumption that FATF-grade AML/CFT compliance is not optional — because the country's own international standing depends on demonstrating it continuously, not just at the moment of grey-list exit.

## Deadlines that have already passed, and what's still coming

- The transitional **No-Objection Certificate (NOC)** window for existing crypto businesses to begin the licensing process reportedly **closed on September 5, 2026**.
- **First full PVARA licenses** are expected in **Q4 2026**.
- **Travel Rule enforcement and deeper FMU integration** are expected to mature through **2027**.
- Operating a virtual asset service in Pakistan **without a PVARA license is a criminal offense** — reported penalty figures vary by source (one guide cites fines up to roughly $179,000 USD equivalent and up to five years' imprisonment; another cites up to PKR 25 million and three years for unauthorized offerings or promotions) — confirm current figures directly with PVARA before relying on any secondary summary, including this one.

## What this means in practice

If your business already offers virtual asset services to users in Pakistan, your licensing status with PVARA is not a background compliance detail — it determines whether your banking relationships are even legally possible under the SBP's April 2026 circular, and whether you're operating within or outside the law. If you're a non-crypto Pakistani business weighing whether to accept crypto payments, the honest answer in late 2026 is: build AML/CFT compliance and Travel Rule readiness into that decision from the start, because the regulatory direction here has only moved toward tighter enforcement all year, not looser.

For businesses operating under FATF-aligned regimes elsewhere — the GCC, the EU, the US — the Pakistan case is also a useful preview of where crypto regulation is generally heading: licensing regimes built explicitly around AML/CFT compliance, with security and due-diligence obligations that flow down to every business in the chain, not just the largest players.
