Search "disaster recovery planning" in 2026 and most of what comes back is scoped specifically to ransomware — which makes sense, given how much attention ransomware gets, but it leaves a gap. **A ransomware attack is one specific cause of business disruption. It is not the only one, and a recovery plan built only around it will have no answer for a hardware failure, an extended cloud outage, a key vendor going down, or a fire at your office.**

Our own [ransomware readiness guide](/blog/ransomware-readiness-backup-recovery-guide/) covers that specific case well. This one covers the broader discipline it sits inside: business continuity and disaster recovery (BC/DR) planning, which is cause-agnostic by design.

## The framework: business impact analysis first

The federal government's NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1 lays out a structured contingency-planning process, and while it's written for US federal information systems rather than as a private-business mandate, its underlying logic transfers cleanly to any size organization. The process starts with a **business impact analysis (BIA)** — and this is the step most small businesses skip entirely, jumping straight to "we need backups" without first answering the question a BIA is designed to answer: *what would actually happen, and how fast do we need each system back, if it went down right now?*

A BIA, in practical terms, means going through your systems and, for each one, answering:

- **What's the impact of this system being unavailable?** (Lost revenue, inability to serve customers, compliance exposure, reputational harm — be specific, not just "bad.")
- **How long could we tolerate it being down?** This is your **Recovery Time Objective (RTO)** — the maximum acceptable outage length before the impact becomes unacceptable.
- **How much data could we afford to lose?** This is your **Recovery Point Objective (RPO)** — if your last backup is from last night and something fails at 4pm, you've lost today's data; is that acceptable, or does it need to be closer to zero?

These two numbers — RTO and RPO — are what should actually drive your backup frequency and recovery approach, not the other way around. A nightly backup gives you an RPO of up to 24 hours; if that's not acceptable for a given system, the backup strategy needs to change, not the expectation.

## Why "beyond ransomware" matters

A plan built narrowly around ransomware recovery tends to assume a specific shape: data is encrypted, you restore from backup, you're done. That assumption doesn't hold for other causes of disruption:

- **Hardware failure** doesn't involve an attacker, but still requires knowing your RTO/RPO and having a tested path to restore service — the "how" is different (no need to verify backups are clean of malware) but the planning discipline is identical.
- **Extended cloud or SaaS provider outages** are increasingly common as businesses depend on more third-party services; your continuity plan needs an answer for "what do we do if [critical vendor] is down for six hours" that doesn't assume the problem is on your own systems at all.
- **Key vendor or supplier failure** — not a cyber event at all, but a business continuity problem with the same shape: what's the impact, how long can you absorb it, what's the fallback.
- **Physical disruption** (fire, flood, extended power outage, a building becoming inaccessible) requires planning that pure data-backup thinking doesn't cover: where do people work, how do they access systems, what's genuinely essential versus nice-to-have in that scenario.

A BC/DR plan built around impact and recovery objectives, rather than a specific cause, has an answer for all of these using the same structure — you're not maintaining four separate playbooks for four different disaster types.

## The three-phase plan structure

NIST SP 800-34's format frames recovery as three phases, which is a useful structure to borrow regardless of cause:

1. **Notification and activation** — how does the organization learn a disruption has happened, and who decides the contingency plan is being activated? Vague answers here ("someone will notice") cost real time during an actual event.
2. **Recovery** — the actual steps to restore operations to the level defined by your RTO/RPO, with specific, pre-assigned responsibility for each step rather than figuring it out in the moment.
3. **Reconstitution** — returning to normal operations once the immediate recovery is complete, including validating that systems are fully functional and capturing what was learned for the next revision of the plan.

## A practical starting BIA for a small business

You don't need the full federal apparatus to get real value from this method. A usable starting point:

1. **List your systems** — not exhaustively, just the ones that matter: email, your website, your core business application, your accounting/financial system, your primary cloud storage.
2. **For each, write down the honest impact of a full-day outage.** Be specific — "we can't invoice clients" is more useful than "it would be bad."
3. **Assign an RTO and RPO to each**, based on that impact — not based on what your current backup happens to provide, based on what the business actually needs.
4. **Compare your current recovery capability against those numbers.** This comparison is usually where the gap shows up — most businesses discover their actual RPO (based on real backup frequency) is worse than what the business genuinely needs.
5. **Close the gap** where it exists, prioritizing the systems with the worst impact-to-current-capability mismatch.
6. **Test it.** A plan that's never been tested is a plan you don't actually know works — this is the same principle our ransomware readiness guide covers for backup testing specifically, and it applies here just as much.

## Business continuity planning checklist

- [ ] List your critical systems and the honest impact of each being unavailable for a day
- [ ] Set an RTO and RPO for each based on business impact, not current backup capability
- [ ] Compare current recovery capability against those targets and identify the gap
- [ ] Document the three phases — notification/activation, recovery, reconstitution — with assigned responsibility, not vague ownership
- [ ] Include non-cyber causes explicitly: vendor outage, physical disruption, hardware failure
- [ ] Test the plan at least annually, more often for your highest-impact systems
- [ ] Revisit the whole BIA after any significant change to systems, vendors, or the business itself

## Key takeaways

Business continuity and disaster recovery planning is the broader discipline that ransomware-specific recovery planning sits inside — cause-agnostic, built around a business impact analysis that produces concrete recovery time and recovery point objectives, then a structured three-phase plan (notification, recovery, reconstitution) to meet them. NIST SP 800-34's framework is written for federal systems, but its underlying logic — impact drives objectives, objectives drive the plan — applies to a business of any size, without needing to adopt it as a compliance mandate.
