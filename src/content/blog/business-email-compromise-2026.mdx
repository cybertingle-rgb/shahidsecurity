Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is, correctly, the standard advice for stopping credential-based attacks — and it works, for most of them. But "most" isn't "all," and the gap between those two words is exactly where a growing share of business email compromise (BEC) activity now lives. Understanding how attackers get around MFA — rather than assuming it's an unbeatable wall — is what actually keeps a business protected.

## The scale, per Microsoft's own 2026 data

Microsoft's threat intelligence team publishes a quarterly email threat landscape report, and the 2026 numbers are large by any measure. In Q1 2026, Microsoft detected approximately **8.3 billion phishing emails**. That volume dropped somewhat to **7.6 billion in Q2 2026**, coinciding with Microsoft's disruption of a major phishing-as-a-service platform known as Tycoon2FA — a takedown that produced a measurable 15% decrease in phishing volume and forced attackers to shift tactics.

Business email compromise specifically accounted for roughly **10.7 million attacks in Q1 2026** alone. Interestingly, the content of most of these wasn't the obvious "urgent wire transfer" scam you might picture — Microsoft's data shows **82–84% of BEC content was generic outreach**, with explicit financial or document requests making up only 9–10%. In other words, most attacks start as a plausible, low-pressure message intended to start a conversation and build trust, with the actual ask coming later, once the target has already engaged.

## How MFA actually gets bypassed

MFA remains genuinely effective against the most common attack pattern: an attacker who has stolen a password (through a data breach, reuse, or a basic phishing page) and tries to log in with it. Without the second factor, that login fails. The bypass techniques that matter operate differently:

- **Adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing**, the technique behind platforms like Tycoon2FA, works by proxying the _real_ login session in real time. The victim lands on a page that looks identical to their actual login screen, enters their password, and completes their real MFA prompt — but the attacker's server sits between the victim and the real service, capturing the resulting authenticated session token. From the service's point of view, this looks like a normal, successful, MFA-verified login, because in a technical sense, it was — just relayed through an attacker.
- **MFA fatigue** relies on repeatedly sending push notification approval requests until a tired or distracted user approves one just to make it stop, without checking whether they actually initiated the login.
- **Social engineering the reset process**, rather than attacking the login itself — convincing a help desk or the user to reset MFA entirely by impersonating IT support or the account owner.

None of these are "MFA doesn't work." They're evidence that MFA shifts the attack from stealing a password to hijacking a session or manipulating a person, which is a real improvement — but not the end of the story.

## The shift beyond email itself

Microsoft's Q2 2026 data also flags a tactical shift worth knowing about specifically because it's newer and less anticipated: as organisations get better at filtering email-based phishing, attackers have expanded into **Microsoft Teams-based social engineering**, including a reported roughly **tenfold increase in voice-based ("vishing") attempts** conducted through Teams calls. The logic is straightforward — email gets filtered and flagged more aggressively than it used to, so attackers move to channels with less mature filtering and a higher baseline of trust (an internal chat platform feels safer to employees than an unsolicited email, even when it shouldn't).

## Security recommendations

- **Use phishing-resistant MFA where possible** — hardware security keys or platform authenticators (like passkeys) that are cryptographically bound to the legitimate site, rather than a code or push approval that can be relayed through a proxy.
- **Train staff to verify unusual requests through a second channel**, especially anything involving payment, credential changes, or urgent action — a quick phone call to a known number catches almost everything AiTM phishing is designed to slip past.
- **Extend phishing awareness to Teams and other internal chat tools**, not just email. If your training only ever mentions "suspicious emails," you've left the fastest-growing channel uncovered.
- **Tighten help desk identity verification for MFA resets.** A process that resets MFA based on a phone call and a name is a bypass waiting to be used.
- **Monitor for impossible or unusual sign-in patterns** — a session originating from an unexpected location or device shortly after a legitimate-looking login is a strong signal, even when MFA was technically satisfied.

## Key takeaways

Business email compromise in 2026 isn't primarily about attackers cracking passwords — it's about patiently building trust through generic outreach, then either relaying a real login session past MFA or moving the conversation to a less-scrutinised channel like Teams. MFA remains essential and blocks the majority of attempts, but treating it as a complete solution — rather than one strong layer among several — is exactly the gap current attackers are trained to find.
