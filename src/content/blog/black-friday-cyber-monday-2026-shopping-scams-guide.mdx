Black Friday and Cyber Monday are also, reliably, the two highest-volume weeks of the year for retail-themed scams — and the pattern repeating in 2026 has two things going for attackers that didn't exist a few years ago: **a sharp rise in convincing fake storefronts, and AI-written scam content that no longer reads like a scam.**

## The fake delivery text

The most common lure remains the simplest one: a text message claiming to be from USPS, UPS, FedEx, or a similar carrier, reporting a missed delivery or a small fee required to release a package. The link leads to a credential-harvesting or payment page styled to look exactly like the real carrier's site. The reliable tell hasn't changed: **a legitimate carrier does not text you to "verify" a tracking number, an address, or a payment detail** — check delivery status only through the retailer's own confirmation email or the carrier's own app or site, typed in directly rather than reached through a link.

## The fake storefront

Security researchers tracked a sharp seasonal spike in fake and malicious shopping sites this year, including lookalike pages specifically impersonating well-known retailers like eBay and Amazon — convincing enough, with real-looking branding and fake reviews, that price alone is no longer a reliable giveaway. These sites typically surface through paid social media ads and sponsored search results rather than organic search, which is itself a useful filter: a deal reached through an ad you didn't go looking for deserves more scrutiny than one found on a retailer's own site.

## What AI changed this year

Two distinct shifts:

- **Scam content quality jumped.** The broken English, generic "Dear Customer" greetings, and awkward phrasing that used to be a fast way to spot a scam are increasingly absent, because the scam emails and texts themselves are now generated by AI. The practical defense shifts from "look for bad writing" to "look for the structural red flags instead" — urgency, a request to act through a link rather than a known app or site, and any request for payment by gift card.
- **AI shopping agents are a new, genuinely unfamiliar attack surface.** As AI agents that browse and check out on a shopper's behalf become more common, several major banks have reportedly raised early concerns about fraud attempts that specifically target or impersonate these agents — a risk category that didn't meaningfully exist a year or two ago. Shahid Security covered the merchant-side version of this shift in our [agentic commerce security guide](/blog/agentic-commerce-security-ai-shopping-agents/); the consumer-side version is new enough that caution, not confidence, is the right default for now.

## What this means beyond your own wallet

If you run a business, this season carries two separate risks worth separating clearly:

- **Employee-side risk**: personal holiday shopping on a work laptop, or a personal delivery-scam text answered during work hours on a device that also holds corporate email, is a realistic path for credential theft or malware to reach systems that have nothing to do with the original purchase. The scam doesn't check whose time or whose device it landed on.
- **Brand-impersonation risk**: if you run an online store, this exact season is when lookalike-domain scams impersonating *your* storefront peak too — fake "too good to be true" versions of your own sales, aimed at your customers. Our [e-commerce security guide](/blog/ecommerce-security-skimming-fraud-guide/) covers defending your actual checkout; watching for impersonation of your brand elsewhere is a separate, worthwhile check this time of year.

## How to actually protect yourself and your team

- **Go direct, not through a link.** Check order status, delivery status, and account "problems" only by typing the retailer's or carrier's own address into your browser or opening their app — never by tapping a link in an unexpected text or email.
- **Pay by credit card, never gift card.** Any request for gift card payment is an immediate, unambiguous red flag regardless of how convincing the surrounding story is.
- **Treat urgency itself as a signal.** "Act now or lose the deal/package/refund" is a manufactured pressure tactic as often as it's real, and AI-polished writing means you can no longer rely on tone alone to tell the difference.
- **Be specifically cautious with new AI shopping-agent tools**, since both the tools and the fraud targeting them are still immature — verify a purchase actually completed through your own account or email confirmation, not just the agent's say-so.
- **Keep personal holiday shopping off managed work devices** where your organization has that policy, and remind staff why, briefly, rather than assuming the policy alone does the work.

## Holiday shopping security checklist

- [ ] Verify delivery and account notices by going directly to the retailer/carrier's site or app, never via a text or email link
- [ ] Treat any gift-card payment request as a confirmed scam, no exceptions
- [ ] Double-check that a "deal" reached through a social media ad is actually on the retailer's real domain before entering payment details
- [ ] Remind staff not to use managed work devices for personal holiday shopping
- [ ] If you run an online store, search periodically this season for lookalike domains or ads impersonating your own brand

## Key takeaways

The individual scam types this Black Friday and Cyber Monday aren't new — fake delivery texts and fake storefronts have been around for years. What's changed is the quality: AI-written scam content that no longer looks like a scam, and a sharp rise in convincing lookalike shopping sites. The defense that still works is structural rather than stylistic — go direct, never pay by gift card, and treat manufactured urgency as a red flag regardless of how polished the message sounds. For businesses, the same season is worth a brief, deliberate reminder to staff, since a personal scam click doesn't stay personal once it lands on a shared device.
