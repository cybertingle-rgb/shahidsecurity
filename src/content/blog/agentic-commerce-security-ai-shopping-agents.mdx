For as long as e-commerce has existed, the assumption behind almost every checkout flow has been simple: a human is on the other end, clicking buttons and typing card numbers. In 2026, that assumption started breaking down. AI agents — acting through new standards like OpenAI and Stripe's **Agentic Commerce Protocol (ACP)** and Google's **Agent Payments Protocol (AP2)** — are now browsing, comparing, and checking out on behalf of real shoppers, and the security model most online stores are running was never built with that in mind.

## What agentic commerce actually is

Agentic commerce describes a shopper delegating part or all of the buying process to an AI agent: "find me a reasonably priced pair of running shoes in my size and buy them" becomes a task the agent completes end-to-end, including the checkout step, rather than a search the human finishes themselves. Two protocols have emerged as the early standards behind this:

- **ACP (Agentic Commerce Protocol)** — built by OpenAI and Stripe, it defines how an AI agent can browse a merchant's catalog and complete checkout in a structured way, and is the standard behind shopping features inside AI assistants like ChatGPT.
- **AP2 (Agent Payments Protocol)** — Google's payments and trust layer, built around cryptographically verifiable "mandates" that are intended to prove an agent is actually authorized to spend on a specific user's behalf, for a specific purpose and limit.

Both are still early, and adoption is concentrated among larger platforms and retailers so far — but the direction is clear enough that any store with an online checkout should understand what changes once a share of "customers" are actually software agents acting on a real person's behalf.

## Why this is a genuinely new security problem, not just more bot traffic

Stores already deal with automated traffic — scrapers, credential-stuffing bots, inventory-sniping scripts — and have built fraud and bot-detection systems to handle it, generally by treating non-human-looking behavior as suspicious. Agentic commerce breaks that heuristic cleanly in half: a **legitimate** AI shopping agent, acting on behalf of a real paying customer, produces request patterns that look a lot like the automated traffic those same systems were built to block.

Industry analysis of this emerging risk, including a report from Unit 42 discussed at the 2026 NRF Big Show, frames the core problem as **"Know Your Agent" (KYA)** — the equivalent of Know Your Customer, but for distinguishing a trustworthy, authorized agent from a malicious one impersonating one. The specific risks already being flagged include:

- **Fraud systems misclassifying legitimate agent traffic** as bot abuse, blocking real sales, or conversely failing to flag genuinely malicious automated purchasing dressed up to look like a legitimate agent.
- **Spending-limit and authorization gaps** — AP2's mandate system is specifically designed to address the risk of an agent exceeding what a user actually authorized, but a store relying purely on the protocol without its own validation is trusting a system still being proven out in the real world.
- **Multi-agent coordination risk** — a scenario researchers have already flagged where several agents, each operating within an individually reasonable limit, could collectively exceed a shared budget or exploit a promotional system faster than a human-paced attack ever could.
- **Prompt injection reaching into checkout** — a risk closely related to what we cover in our article on [the risks of agentic AI browsers](/blog/agentic-ai-browser-security-risks/): a compromised or manipulated shopping agent could be tricked into completing a purchase, changing a shipping address, or leaking payment-adjacent data through a maliciously crafted product listing or page content.

## What store owners should actually do now

- **Audit your fraud and bot-detection rules for how they would treat a legitimate, well-behaved automated agent** versus a malicious script — most current systems were tuned for a world where that distinction did not need to exist.
- **Do not treat protocol adoption (ACP, AP2, or similar) as a security feature by itself.** These standards address authorization and structure; your store still needs its own validation, rate limiting, and anomaly detection layered on top, the same way you would not trust a payment processor's integration alone to catch skimming (a risk our [e-commerce security guide](/blog/ecommerce-security-skimming-fraud-guide/) covers in detail).
- **Test your checkout and product pages against prompt injection scenarios** if you have any AI-facing integration or expect agent traffic — a maliciously crafted product review, listing, or page element is now a plausible vector for manipulating an agent's behavior on your own site.
- **Watch your access and request logs for emerging agent-traffic patterns** specifically, rather than lumping all automated traffic into one "bot" bucket — understanding what legitimate agent traffic actually looks like on your store is a prerequisite for ever being able to tell it apart from abuse.
- **Keep a human-reviewable audit trail for high-value or unusual automated transactions**, at least until the fraud tooling built specifically for agentic commerce matures.

## Agentic commerce readiness checklist

- [ ] Review current fraud and bot-detection rules for how they would treat legitimate AI shopping agent traffic
- [ ] Avoid relying on protocol-level authorization (ACP/AP2) alone as your store's only agent-fraud defense
- [ ] Test checkout and product pages against prompt injection attempts targeting an AI shopping agent
- [ ] Establish logging that can distinguish agent traffic patterns from generic bot and scraper traffic
- [ ] Set manual review thresholds for high-value or unusual automated transactions
- [ ] Revisit this review cycle regularly — this is an early-stage, fast-moving standard, not a settled one

## Key takeaways

Agentic commerce is moving from a future concept to a real, if still early, part of how people shop in 2026, through standards like ACP and AP2 that let AI agents browse and check out on a shopper's behalf. The security challenge is not simply "more bots" — it is that legitimate agent traffic and malicious automated traffic can look alike to systems built before this distinction mattered, which means fraud detection, bot defense, and even prompt injection resistance all need a fresh look specifically through this lens, before it becomes a meaningful share of checkout traffic rather than an early edge case.
